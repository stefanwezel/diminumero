# Questions for Welsh speakers

*Follow-up to the r/learnwelsh thread about [diminumero](https://diminumero.com/cy) — a
free, ad-free site for drilling number words.*

Both systems are on the site, switchable with a `Degol` / `Ugeiniol` toggle. Every form
we hold is tagged with where it came from, and that tag decides whether a learner ever
sees it.

---

## Answered: the 41–99 connective

It is **`a`**, with the aspirate mutation, and the evidence was never in conflict.

The aspirate mutation only touches **p, t, c**. `deugain` starts with `d`, so it cannot
show the mutation at all — which is why `deg a thrigain` (70) and `deg a phedwar ugain`
(90) look like a different rule from `tri a deugain` (43). They are the same rule. The
absence of a visible change is a gap in the aspirate inventory, not a second connective.
Wiktionary's usage note at *deugain* states the rule for 41–59 directly and carries
`tri a deugain` as a headword with a 19th-century citation; Cornish splits the same way
(`warn ugens` for 21–39, `ha dew-ugens` for 41), which suggests the split is old.

Three consequences on the site:

* **22–99 are now drilled.** The deck runs 1–100.
* **45 is now `pump a deugain`.** The thread's `pump ar ddeugain` is kept and labelled as
  an attested minority pattern — most likely analogy from the heavily-used `ar hugain`
  run of 21–39 — rather than deleted.
* **A new provenance tier, `attested`**, for a form produced by a rule that a published
  reference states. It sits below "a speaker said it" and above "a script typed it", and
  it is served. Which brings us to the first question.

## 3. Is the drill weighted the way the language is used?

We were told the traditional system is alive up to about 30 and in the 40–70 band, and
thinning generation by generation. So the drill now asks:

* **often** — 1–31 (dates), and the bare scores `deugain`, `hanner cant`, `trigain`,
  `pedwar ugain`, `cant`;
* **sometimes** — the 41–99 compounds, as recognition practice;
* **rarely** — 39, 59, 79, 99, the stacked ones.

Is that the right shape? Which numbers would you move?

## 4. Two forms nobody has confirmed, so the drill skips them

* **120** — `chwe ugain` or `chweugain`? And is the `chweugain` = ten shillings → 50p
  story still current, or a memory?
* **Zero** — we would use `dim`, and `sero` for temperature and maths. Does the
  traditional system have a word for zero at all, or is the question meaningless?

## 5. `deuddeg` or `deudeg` for 12?

We teach `deuddeg` and carry `deudeg` as a colloquial variant, especially for telling the
time. Right way round? Is `deudeg` regional, or just spoken?

## 6. Money

What we say now: money is mostly decimal, but certain amounts have fixed traditional
forms that survive in ordinary speech — `hanner can punt`, `chweugain`. (The earlier
claim that money is traditional up to about £30 is deleted; nobody supported it.) Closer
to the truth? Which fixed forms would you add?

## 7. Feminine forms in a bare-number drill

The drill shows a digit and asks for the word, with no noun attached, so there is nothing
for `dwy` / `tair` / `pedair` to agree with. We show masculine and say so. Is
masculine-by-default right, or does it teach a bad habit?

We do now store both series, including through the compounds — `tri`/`tair ar ddeg` (13),
`dau`/`dwy ar hugain` (22), `pedwar`/`pedair a phedwar ugain` (84), with only the unit
changing and the score left alone.

## 8. Are `Degol` and `Ugeiniol` the right names?

They are the two buttons, untranslated, in every interface language. Are they what you
would put on a button? And how would you explain the difference to a learner in one line?

## 9. Ordinals — worth building next?

Dates are the biggest surviving use of the traditional system, and dates need ordinals
(`y trydydd ar ddeg o Fai`), which we do not produce at all. Before we build it: which
ordinals does a learner actually need — 1st–31st only? Do the feminine forms matter for
dates? And is `yr unfed ar hugain` the everyday way to say 21st?

## 10. Please check these notes

## Notes to ourselves, kept here so they are not lost

**Two sources sharing a mistake are one source.** Omniglot and Parallel.cymru both give
34 as `pedwar deg ar hugain`, which computes to 60; the traditional form is
`pedwar ar ddeg ar hugain` (14 + 20). Their 40–100 rows are otherwise identical in
content and ordering. Neither ever entered our dataset — our tiers count people in the
thread — but the lesson stands: appearing in two places online is not corroboration.

**Third pattern in circulation.** Welsh Wikipedia gives `deugain ac un` for 41 beside the
regular `un a deugain`. Recorded as a reversed-order variant; the regular form is taught.
