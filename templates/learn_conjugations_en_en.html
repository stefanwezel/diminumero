Learn English Verb Conjugations

English verbs change far less than most: the work lies in the auxiliaries and in the irregular past forms. This page covers British English, which is what the practice accepts.

The five persons

English has five person slots rather than six, because you has the same form for one person and for several.

  • I — the speaker
  • you — one person or several, formal or informal
  • he / she / it — one other person or thing
  • we — the speaker and others
  • they — several other people or things

The verb alone rarely shows who is acting, so English always says the pronoun. In the practice the pronoun is given and you type only the verb form: am working, not "I am working".

Present simple: the -s

The present simple is the base form of the verb, but he/she/it adds -s:

  • Most verbs add -s: work → works
  • After s, x, ch, sh and o, add -es: watch → watches, fix → fixes, go → goes
  • Consonant + y becomes -ies: study → studies
  • Vowel + y just adds -s: play → plays

be and have have forms of their own, and modal verbs such as can never change:

  • be: am, are, is, are, are
  • have: have, have, has, have, have
  • can: can, can, can, can, can

Regular past: -ed

Regular verbs add -ed for every person, and the past participle looks the same:

  • Ending in e: add -d: like → liked
  • Consonant + y becomes -ied: study → studied
  • One short, stressed vowel + one consonant: double the consonant: stop → stopped, plan → planned, prefer → preferred
  • An unstressed last syllable does not double: visit → visited, open → opened
  • British English always doubles a final l after a single vowel: travel → travelled, cancel → cancelled

The -ing form

The continuous tenses use the -ing form, and its spelling follows similar rules:

  • Drop a final e: make → making
  • Keep ee: see → seeing
  • ie becomes y: die → dying, lie → lying
  • Double the consonant, as in the past: stop → stopping, travel → travelling

Verbs of state, such as know, want, like, need, believe and understand, are not normally used in the continuous, so the practice never asks for those forms.

The tenses you practise

The practice settings let you mix and match these tenses (examples with work and go):

  • Present simple — habits and facts: work, works
  • Past simple — finished events: worked, went
  • Present continuous — happening right now: am working, is working
  • Present perfect — the past with a link to now: have worked, has gone
  • Future (will) — decisions and predictions: will work
  • Past continuous — in progress at a moment in the past: was working, were working
  • Future (going to) — plans: am going to work, is going to go
  • Conditional (would) — what would happen: would work
  • Past perfect — the past before the past: had worked, had gone
  • Present perfect continuous — started in the past and still going on: have been working, has been working

The practice asks for the full affirmative form: will go, have gone, with no contractions or negation.

Irregular verbs

An irregular verb has to be learned as three forms: the base form, the past simple and the past participle. These come up most:

  • go → went, gone
  • get → got, got
  • make → made, made
  • take → took, taken
  • see → saw, seen
  • come → came, come
  • know → knew, known
  • give → gave, given
  • think → thought, thought
  • write → wrote, written
  • buy → bought, bought
  • put → put, put

In the past simple, only be changes with the person: was for I and he/she/it, were for you, we and they. Its participle is been.

Three patterns recur: all three forms the same (put), past and participle the same (buy), or all three different (write).

British forms

Where British usage itself has two forms, the practice accepts both. American forms are marked wrong.

  • Both accepted: learnt / learned, burnt / burned, dreamt / dreamed, realise / realize
  • Participle of get: have got — "have gotten" is American
  • Double l: travelled — "traveled" is American
  • The verb is spelled with s: practise — "practice" is American

Common mistakes

  1. Forgetting the -s: he works, not "he work".
  2. Past simple instead of the participle: have gone, not "have went".
  3. An -s on a modal: she can, not "she cans".
  4. A verb of state in the continuous: I know, not "I am knowing".
  5. Dropping the auxiliary: am working, not "I working".
